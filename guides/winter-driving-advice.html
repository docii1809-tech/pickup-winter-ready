Cold-Weather Driving Technique

Winter Driving Advice for Pickup Truck Owners

Pickups behave differently from sedans and crossovers on snow and ice. Understanding weight balance, braking distance, and four-wheel-drive limits keeps your truck upright and out of the ditch.

01. Four-Wheel Drive Helps You Go—Not Stop

One of the most expensive winter driving mistakes happens when 4WD pickups pull away smoothly from an icy stoplight, giving the driver false confidence. Every vehicle already has four-wheel brakes. Four-wheel drive does not shorten braking distances on packed snow or black ice, and heavy pickup trucks carry extra momentum into intersections. Leave at least triple your dry-road following distance.

02. Placing Bed Ballast Safely Over the Rear Axle

An empty truck bed leaves the rear tires lightly loaded. Adding roughly 150 to 300 pounds of secured ballast (such as tube sand bags or traction-sand bags) directly over or slightly ahead of the rear axle improves rear tire bite in 2WD and reduces sudden fishtailing.

Important safety rule: Always brace or strap ballast firmly between the wheel wells so heavy sandbags cannot slide forward against the cab bulkhead in a sudden stop. Avoid loose cinder blocks or metal scraps that become hazards in a collision.

03. Part-Time 4WD vs. Auto 4WD on Patchy Pavement

On older pickups with traditional part-time 4H (locked front-to-rear transfer case), avoid driving in 4WD on dry pavement where tight turns cause driveline binding. Use 4H when roads are snow-covered or slushy, and shift back to 2H once pavement clears and dries.

04. Watch Bridges, Overpasses, and Shaded Rural Curves

Bridge decks lose heat from both above and below, freezing long before approach asphalt looks slippery. Ease off the throttle before crossing overpasses—never brake hard or make abrupt steering inputs mid-span. Also disable cruise control on wet, slushy, or sub-freezing highways so the transmission never downshifts unexpectedly on a slick patch.

05. Diesel & Older Daily-Driver Cold-Weather Habits

  • Keep your fuel tank at least half full during storms to prevent condensation freezing in fuel lines and to ensure plenty of reserve if traffic stalls for hours.
  • For diesel pickups in severe cold snaps, ask your fuel station about winter-blended diesel and keep an anti-gel additive in the truck if temperatures plunge suddenly.
  • Clear snow off headlights, taillights, turn signals, and the cab roof before pulling onto the road.

06. If You Slide Off or Get Stuck in Deep Snow

Stay with your vehicle if a storm is active and visibility is poor. Before idling the engine for cabin heat, step out and clear snow away from the exhaust tailpipe so carbon monoxide cannot back up underneath the cab. Run the engine intermittently rather than continuously, crack a downwind window slightly, and put out high-visibility flares or beacons if near a roadway.
